# Code-do: Programming as a Way After the Advent of AI
---
## Where Code-do Begins
The original idea of Code-do was introduced by John Carmack in a post he wrote after reading Miyamoto Musashi’s “The Book of Five Rings”.

What caught his attention was a transformation that martial practices had undergone over time: a technique born from practical necessity could continue to exist after that necessity disappeared. Carmack saw a similar process emerging in programming.

Programmers of older generations remember operations that were once unavoidable if you wanted a working program: manually assembling machine instructions, working without modern compilers, high-level languages, libraries, IDEs, and other layers of automation. Most of those operations are no longer a required part of everyday programming.

Today AI is continuing that process at the level of writing and constructing programs themselves. A growing share of the work that a programmer once had to perform directly can now be delegated to a model or an agent.

This is where Carmack draws the analogy **jutsu → dō**: careful, hands-on coding may shift from a method required to obtain a software result into a practice a person continues voluntarily. Hence his playful question: **Code-do?**

Carmack also places an important limit on the idea. Preserved mastery does not create an advantage by itself, nor is it an argument against a stronger tool. His image of a kung fu master who inherits generations of tradition yet loses to a more modern way of fighting directly warns against that mistake. Musashi, Carmack jokes, would probably have been quite interested in an assault rifle.

The value of an older practice lies in the function it preserves after a stronger tool appears. If the new tool solves the production task better, the difficulty of the older method does not grant it special status.

But Carmack’s analogy reaches much further than its brief formulation. It joins two concepts whose meaning is barely developed in the post itself: **programming** and **dō**.

Carmack outlined a possible shape, but did not define it. To find out whether Code-do is more than a striking metaphor, two fundamental questions have to be answered first – **and from this point on, the article is the authors’ interpretation.**

First: **what exactly did programmers create, and what place has programming come to occupy in modern civilization?**

If a programmer is seen only as a specialist who writes software, automating that work looks like yet another technological replacement of one professional skill by another. But that frame says nothing about the historical function of the class of people who created the executable logic of the digital world.

Second: **what exactly does dō mean in the culture from which Carmack borrowed the analogy?**

If dō is read merely as a hobby, a craft, or the voluntary continuation of an obsolete technique, the idea of Code-do loses most of its meaning. “The Way” has another function, and without understanding it we cannot see what programming could become within this analogy.

Without the first answer, we do not know **what is moving into a new state**.

Without the second, **we do not know what it is moving into**.

Before speaking about Code-do, then, we need to establish what programming has become for modern digital civilization and what place the programmer occupies within it.

## The Programmer’s Place in Digital Civilization
Programming joined the formal description of rules, dependencies, operations, and sequences with a crucial property: a formal construction could now be executed by a machine.

A mathematical expression describes a dependency. An algorithm specifies a sequence of actions. A logical system establishes rules for moving from one defined state to another. Program code transfers such relations into a computing system that executes them, processes data, changes its own state, or triggers action in the real world.

In this sense, **code is formalized logic that has been given the ability to act**.

This property made programming far more than a narrow technical profession. The digitalization of modern civilization rests on the ability to turn rules, decisions, and processes into executable program logic.

As a result, **program logic becomes part of the mechanism by which civilizational processes are organized and carried out**.

Program logic has gradually entered almost every major sphere of modern civilization. It supports global communication and access to information, the movement of money and trade, public registries and digital services, education, scientific computing, and professional tools. **What once required direct human coordination is increasingly carried out through software systems.**

The same transition has taken place in the physical world: transport, logistics, industry, energy, agriculture, medicine, space systems, security, and defense. **Code controls machines, processes signals and data, coordinates routes and supply chains, supports infrastructure, and connects digital decisions with physical processes.**

**Perhaps the clearest result of this concentration is the smartphone: a small device in a pocket has become a personal terminal for access to communication, money, documents, commerce, navigation, public services, entertainment, and an ever larger share of digital civilization.**

The most important level appears when these systems begin to work together. A bank interacts with a store and public systems; transport with navigation and logistics; medicine with insurers and public registries; the military with communications, space systems, industry, and supply. Software has become one of the main mechanisms for coordinating enormous numbers of people, organizations, machines, and processes within a shared digital environment.

Behind all this diversity lies one fundamental transition: part of the logic by which civilization functions has been transformed into executable program logic. Software increasingly governs what data is available, the sequence in which a process unfolds, which states a system allows, which rules are triggered, and which actions become possible.

Many different specialists built this layer. Engineers built computing and network infrastructure; mathematicians and scientists developed models and algorithms; domain experts defined rules and requirements. The programmer’s specific function became turning those rules, processes, and models into the executable logic of software systems.

**It is through this function that programmers became one of the foundational classes of modern digital civilization.**

And yet their role is almost invisible. A person sees a banking app, a navigator, or a government service, but usually not the logic that governs its behavior. The more reliably software works, the less reason a user has to think about how it is built.

This is one of the paradoxes of digitalization: **program logic became more fundamental to everyday life precisely as it became less visible to most people**.

The programmer’s distinctive ability remained equally invisible. Building a digital system requires more than knowing a programming language. A vague intention has to be turned into an unambiguous construction: objects, states, dependencies, rules, sequences, boundaries, and exceptions must be defined; the consequences of decisions anticipated; actual system behavior tested; the source of an error located; and the logic rebuilt when the result diverges from what was expected.

**As a user, a person acts inside logic that has already been created. Programming ability makes it possible to read, create, test, and change that logic.**

This is what makes the programmer’s historical role fundamental. Civilization needed the programmer not merely as someone who writes code, but as a **bearer of the ability to turn human rules, processes, knowledge, and decisions into executable logic capable of controlling digital systems and coordinating the physical world**.

**This is what stands behind the word Code in Code-do.**

Now that we have defined what stands behind the word Code, we need to define the second part just as precisely: what does dō – the Way – mean in the culture from which the concept comes?

## What dō – the Way – Means
The history of the concept of dō is much older than its association with Japanese martial arts.

The character 道 comes from the Chinese cultural tradition, where dao meant a path, direction, mode of action, and order of passage. It entered Japan with Chinese writing and cultural vocabulary around the fifth and sixth centuries. Japanese already had its own word, michi – path or way – while the borrowed reading dō gradually entered the same broader field of meaning.

Over time, “way” came to name a field of knowledge or mastery that a person gradually learned to inhabit. In medieval Japan, michi and dō increasingly acquired the sense of a **way of going through and mastering a particular practice**.

By the fifteenth century, this construction was already visible as a Way of mastery. In the work of Noh master **Zeami Motokiyo**, art appears as a long practice extending across different stages of life. The practitioner observes, repeats, performs, receives correction, accumulates experience, and gradually changes their own capacity to act.

Here an important relation characteristic of dō becomes visible: **going through the practice forms the practitioner**.

In the centuries that followed, this model developed across different Japanese arts, crafts, and schools. The specific forms differed, but one structure repeated: **long practice, discipline, correction, accumulated mastery, and the transmission of a way of practice to those who came next**.

The warrior tradition entered this cultural field later. By the late sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, different concepts of the warrior’s way, including bushidō and shidō, were spreading. With the establishment of prolonged peace, the warrior’s function gradually extended beyond direct combat and became increasingly associated with discipline of conduct, service, responsibility, and self-formation.

In the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, this line took the modern form of budō. Older martial practices were reinterpreted as Ways in which technical mastery was joined by the formative function of the practice itself.

**The transition jutsu → dō therefore became one of the later expressions of this much older cultural construction.**

The historical form changed, but across different manifestations of the Way a common functional structure can be traced.

A Way exists through practice extended over time. A single action ends with its result. A practitioner returns to the Way again, now carrying the experience of the previous passage.

That is why practice produces two connected results. **The first is external: an action performed, an object created, or a problem solved. The second is a change in the practitioner’s own capacity.**

Repeated passage allows a person to distinguish relations more precisely, see their own errors, anticipate consequences, and work with more complex problems. Testing the result and correcting one’s own action turns repetition into accumulated experience.

**This is how mastery is formed.**

Mastery does not end the Way. A developed capacity changes what the practitioner can see and work with in the next passage. What once demanded all of their attention becomes the basis for more complex work.

A Way can also be transmitted between people. Passing on a finished result or a description of the correct action is not enough. The method of passage itself has to be preserved so that the next practitioner can form the corresponding capacity for themselves. This is the function served by discipline, exercises, teachers, schools, and traditions of transmission.

From this we can formulate the meaning of dō that Code-do needs:\
**Dō is a Way of sustained practice in which going through the practice itself forms the practitioner.**

**Through repetition, experience, correction, and deepening of the practice, a person’s capacity to act within that Way changes.**

Technical output remains part of such a Way. Alongside it, another result appears: a human capacity formed through practice.

That is why historical age, difficulty, or tradition alone do not define dō. What matters is the function of the passage: **what exactly the practice forms in a person, and how the formed capacity develops through subsequent passages**.

**The second part of Code-do is now defined.**

Code is executable logic through which programmers helped build digital civilization.

Dō is a Way in which systematic passage through practice forms the practitioner’s capacity.

This leads to the next question: **what exactly does the direct practice of creating program logic form in a person, and what happens to that mechanism of formation when AI takes over an ever larger share of constructing that logic?**

## What AI Changes
Programming has been automated throughout its history: more and more intermediate operations between intention and result have moved into tools. But the programmer remained the person who received a task and built the concrete logic that solved it.

Modern AI became possible inside the same digital civilization that programmers built over decades alongside engineers, mathematicians, scientists, and other specialists. Software, languages, tools, computing infrastructure, and machine-learning systems themselves became part of the environment in which AI could emerge and operate.

At the same time, generations of programmers accumulated an enormous body of code and recorded experience of creating it. That human material became one of the foundations on which modern models developed the ability to read code, find errors, and create new program logic.

This creates an important historical sequence: before a machine could create program logic, generations of people first had to create that logic, test it, correct it, and accumulate the results of their work.

Now AI is beginning to take over part of the very practice through which generations of programmers developed the ability to work with program logic.

Modern models and agents can already, in many programming tasks, receive a description of the desired result, build an implementation, run the code, test it, find errors, and make changes.

Automation is entering the process of constructing logic itself.

A person can increasingly move from the position:\
**“I create the logic of the system”**\
to the position:\
**“I define what I want to obtain, and the system creates the logic of implementation”.**

For software production, this kind of automation can provide major advantages in speed, cost, and scale. If one person working with an agent can do work that once required a team, software can be produced faster, more cheaply, and at much greater scale.

**But the civilizational consequence of this transition is broader than a change in programmer productivity.**

Before this level of automation, creating new program logic remained directly tied to the work of people capable of designing, implementing, testing, correcting, and maintaining it. Earlier generations of automation reduced the amount of that work, but constructing the concrete logic of a system remained predominantly a human task.

AI creates a different possibility:\
**the amount of executable digital logic can continue to grow without a proportional growth in the number of people capable of creating that logic independently.**

**This is a fundamental change.**

A situation can therefore emerge in which civilization’s dependence on software systems continues to grow while the professional necessity to create their logic directly applies to an ever smaller share of people.

Using AI does not mean controlling all the logic of the system through which a task is carried out. A user may define a local objective, but the model, available data and tools, permitted actions, constraints, and priorities already define the space in which that objective can be realized.

**The user defines the local objective. The architecture of the system defines the space within which that objective can be realized.**

AI responses arise dynamically within a system architecture, while a significant part of the structure in which that process occurs is created and controlled at other levels.

Mass delegation of programming to AI can therefore change far more than the professional structure of the software industry.

The broad layer of people for whom directly creating, reading, testing, and rebuilding program logic was a necessary part of professional activity may shrink.

The digital world itself, however, will continue to be made of precisely this kind of logic.

A new asymmetry appears: **a person may live inside an increasingly complex system of rules created by code while becoming progressively less able to independently read, test, or create a comparable system**.

This does not prevent a person from using digital tools successfully, assigning tasks to AI, and obtaining high-quality results. But the space of actions available to them remains defined by the logic of systems created and controlled at other levels.

As long as direct programming remained a necessary part of building digital systems, the profession also reproduced a large class of people who continuously practiced constructing executable logic.

If AI reduces the production need for that direct passage, another question appears: **does the practice of programming itself have value independently of the need to obtain a software result?**

Such value appears when the result of practice is not only the program that was created, but also the capacity a person forms by going through the practice itself.

This is where programming can be seen not only as a way to build digital systems, but as a Way for forming the practitioner.

## The Idea of Code-do
This possibility forms the basis of the idea of Code-do.

**The idea of Code-do is to use the practice of programming to form in a person the capacity to independently create, read, test, and rebuild logic when professional practice no longer reliably forms that capacity**.

Core:\
**Code-do is a Way for forming a bearer of logic through code.**

This idea begins with a distinctive property of programming itself.

A person intends to produce a behavior that does not yet exist. To realize it, they must define the task, break it into parts, establish relations, rules, states, and sequences of actions, and then turn that construction into code.

**Once it is run, the logic begins to act.**

It returns a result that no longer depends on what the programmer intended to mean. The system executes the construction that was actually created. If the result diverges from expectation, the point of failure has to be found: a false assumption, a missing relation, an unaccounted-for state, an incorrect sequence, or a rule that produced a different consequence.

The logic is then rebuilt and tested again.

Programming therefore continually reproduces one fundamental cycle:\
**intention → construction of logic → execution → consequence → verification → locating the break → reconstruction of logic.**

In this cycle, code is more than a means of recording a solution. It creates an environment in which logic built by a person is given the ability to act and, by acting, to test its author.

From this follows the central thesis of Code-do: going through this kind of practice forms the capacity to work independently with logic where no ready-made construction yet exists.

**Through such practice, a person learns to identify relations, see dependencies, trace consequences, find contradictions, reconstruct other people’s decisions, and rebuild a system when its previous logic stops working.**

This is how a **bearer of logic** is formed.

A bearer of logic is a person capable of independently constructing executable logic for a new problem, tracing the relation between its rules and consequences, testing the behavior that was created, locating a break, and rebuilding the construction.

In complex systems, including AI, the internal mechanics of an individual component may be unavailable for direct tracing. In that case, a bearer of logic works with relations that can be defined and tested at the level of the system and its observable consequences.

This capacity matters far beyond professional software development. People constantly act inside banking systems, platforms, government services, algorithms, automated processes, and a growing number of AI systems. Each has its own rules, priorities, boundaries, permitted actions, and mechanisms for making decisions.

The practice of creating logic changes how a person interacts with such systems.

Before accepting a ready-made result, a question appears:\
**why?**\
Why did the system do exactly this?\
Which relation produced this consequence?\
Which rule shaped the available options?\
Where is the boundary of what is possible?\
Who or what sets the priority?\
What assumption is hidden inside the construction?\
What changes if the relation itself is rebuilt?

The question **“why?”** moves a person from using a ready-made result to searching for the logic that produced it. And the ability to create logic makes the next step possible: seeing an alternative construction.

This is why programming can retain value after its production role changes.

Its practice can form a person who works with the digital world at the level of its logic, rather than only at the level of the interface made available to them.

From the formation of this capacity follows the next question: **how is it preserved and reproduced across generations of practitioners?**

Modern programming rests on the experience of people who spent decades building systems, breaking them, finding the causes of errors, rebuilding architectures, and developing methods for testing, security, reliability, and managing complexity. Each new generation inherited ready-made languages, tools, and principles, while still going through its own practice of creating program logic.

Part of practical knowledge therefore existed not only in documentation, code, or textbooks, but was reproduced in people who went through the relevant practice themselves.

AI changes the conditions of this transmission. When an automated system takes over a significant share of constructing, testing, and correcting logic, accumulated experience may remain available in models, documentation, and ready-made solutions, while the path by which humans reproduce that experience is no longer reliably sustained by professional practice.

For Code-do, **continuity in transmitting the experience of creating logic** therefore becomes more important than preserving particular old technologies.

The transmission of this capacity can also produce a broader social consequence.

If the capacity to work independently with executable logic continues to be reproduced among independent practitioners, digital society retains a broader human layer capable of working with the logical foundations of its systems.

In an environment where an increasing share of program logic is created automatically, such competence may matter for independent system verification, exposing hidden assumptions, finding accumulated errors, and rebuilding constructions that create critical risks.

**The core of Code-do nevertheless remains the formation of the individual practitioner. Transmission allows this capacity to persist over time, while sufficiently broad reproduction can create a social effect: distributed human competence in working with executable logic.**

This effect belongs to the competence itself, not to a particular generation, professional status, or image of the “old school”.

**When professional practice no longer reliably forms a bearer of logic, Code-do preserves programming practice as a distinct Way for forming one.**

Code remains the material of the practice. Its result becomes the human capacity to create, read, test, and rebuild logic.

The idea is defined.

## What It Takes for the Idea to Become a Discipline
The idea of Code-do defines the function of the Way: **forming a bearer of logic through code**. But a defined function does not yet create a discipline.

For Code-do to become a discipline, it needs a reproducible mechanism of practice capable of forming the stated capacity in different people and making it possible to verify whether that capacity has actually formed.

**The philosophical level defines the meaning, purpose, and fundamental grounds of the practice.** The discipline must specify the forms of passage through which that function can be realized and tested.

The future practice of Code-do must therefore be built around the specific capacity it is meant to form.

In the previous section we identified a form of passage characteristic of programming: a person builds a logical construction, lets it execute, receives a real consequence, and uses that consequence to test their own decision.

If this kind of passage forms the required capacity, **the future discipline must preserve its function even as particular tools, languages, and levels of automation change**.

From this follows one requirement for the future discipline: it must identify **which part of the passage a person has to perform independently for the required capacity to actually form**. Supporting operations can be automated, but delegation must not remove the very experience for which the specific practice was designed.

**This makes error a full part of the discipline.**

An incorrect construction produces an observable consequence. The practitioner can trace it back to their own decision, locate the point of failure, and rebuild the logic. A correct result alone therefore does not show how it was obtained or whether the required capacity was formed. Working through one’s own error provides a separate test: can the practitioner trace the consequence back to its cause and rebuild their own decision?

Code-do practice must provide room for this kind of passage. A person must have the opportunity to make an error through their own logic, see the consequence of their own decision, and independently find a way to correct it.

**But successfully completing a particular exercise still does not prove that the capacity has formed.**

The real test comes when a person encounters another problem, another structure, and other constraints, yet can still reproduce the same mode of logical work.

**For a future Code-do discipline, one of the key criteria of a formed capacity should be its transfer to a new situation.**

This also defines the place of repeatable exercises. Code-do may include **kata** – deliberately constructed forms of practice that make a person repeatedly go through a particular kind of logical work. Kata gives structure to repetition, but the result remains a capacity that must also work outside the exercise itself.

If a person learns to repeat a kata flawlessly but loses the corresponding way of thinking when the problem changes, they have mastered the form without the function.

If Code-do develops into a full discipline, several levels will need to be distinguished in its construction.

**Code-do Philosophy** defines what this Way is, where its meaning and value lie, and which fundamental principles must remain stable regardless of the specific forms of practice.\
**Code-do Discipline** defines which capacity is to be formed, through which principles and forms of passage it is formed, and by what criteria the result is tested.\
**Code-do Practice** is a concrete passage in which a person performs the actions necessary to form or test the defined capacity.\
**Code-do Kata** is a defined repeatable form of practice built for repeatedly going through a particular kind of logical work in order to form a specific capacity.

This structure also defines the place of Code-do in relation to ordinary programming education.

Programming education transmits the knowledge, methods, and skills needed to build software systems. Code-do uses the same programming material for a different final function: **code becomes an environment for forming a bearer of logic**.

A particular exercise does not become Code-do practice by itself. Its place follows from its function: **what capacity does it form, and does that capacity actually transfer beyond the exercise itself?**

This also matters for transmitting Code-do to future generations. The discipline must preserve the method for designing practices around the required capacity, while the specific exercises can change with the technological environment.

We can therefore define the minimum requirements a future Code-do discipline must meet: it must define the required capacity, create practice for forming it, produce a verifiable result, and establish whether the formed capacity transfers to new problems.

These requirements are not yet a finished methodology. The concrete practices, verification criteria, and forms of transmission of Code-do still require separate development and practical testing.

The role of AI in such a discipline depends on the function of each specific practice. A tool may perform part of the work as long as the practice preserves the passage required to form the target capacity. Where exactly that boundary lies is a question for the construction and testing of the Code-do discipline itself.

## Code-do: A Change in the Function of Programming
The emergence of AI changes the conditions of human participation in programming.

An increasing share of the path from intention to finished program logic can pass through automated systems. At the same time, professional activity may cease to provide what had been its by-product for decades: a person repeatedly going through the process of constructing, executing, testing, and rebuilding program logic themselves.

**This is where another function opens up for programming.**

Historically, direct human practice was necessary to create a large share of digital systems. AI weakens the direct link between a human going through that practice and obtaining a software result. At the same time, digital systems continue to operate through rules, states, dependencies, priorities, and decisions that program logic gives the ability to act.

Alongside the production function of programming, another value can therefore emerge: **forming and preserving the human capacity to work with the logic from which digital systems are built**.

This is the central meaning of the transition to Code-do.

**Code-do is a Way for forming the capacity to create, read, test, and rebuild logic through the practice of programming.**

In this definition, code is the material of the Way: an environment in which logic can be formulated, run, observed through its consequences, broken open at the point of failure, and rebuilt.

**And the final bearer of the result is the human being.**

This also defines what is transmitted between generations. What must be passed on is not a particular technological form, but the capacity the practice forms. **The continuity of Code-do lies in reproducing that capacity under new technological conditions.**

Sufficiently broad reproduction of this capacity can also produce a social consequence: distributed human competence capable of working independently with the logical foundations of digital systems. **This is not a separate goal of Code-do, but a possible consequence of sufficiently broad diffusion of the capacity it forms.**

The future of Code-do will therefore depend on its ability to preserve the function of the Way under conditions of continuous technological change.

Languages will change. Levels of abstraction will rise. Some operations will disappear from human practice. AI will take over more and more work. The forms of Code-do must change with them. The main criterion for those forms must remain their function: **whether the practice forms a person capable of working independently with logic, or merely reproduces a form that once formed such a capacity**.

For that reason, the concrete practices of Code-do still need to be created and tested by people with sufficient programming mastery to do that work. The idea defines the function of the Way and the requirements for a future discipline. **The discipline itself must still prove in practice that it can perform that function.**

**The original analogy of the transition from jutsu to dō reveals a much larger change.**

Programming may gradually lose its status as the necessary human path to a software result while gaining another reason to remain a human practice.

When machines take over an ever larger share of creating code, the value of the Way itself may shift toward what that path forms in the human being.

**It is at this point that Code finds its dō.**
